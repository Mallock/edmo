A companion tool for Elite Dangerous commanders

A mission HUD
with a voice.

A small always-on-top panel that reads your Player Journal as you fly: mission cards with the objective checklist the game never gives you, live expiry timers, and an operator that notices when you have stalled and says so — out loud, in a neural voice synthesized on your own machine.

Ask it anything and the answer comes from your real missions, your real hold and the market you are actually docked at. Everything runs here — including the AI, which it installs itself.

01

What it does

module manifest

What it reads the journal is the whole input — nothing is guessed

Live mission tracking

Reads the game's own journal files as you play. Cards show category, destination, reward, kill and cargo counters, gift commodities for VIPs, and a synthesized objective checklist the game never gives you — with live expiry timers. It reads the whole board: couriers and cargo, passengers and sightseeing, massacres and assassinations, plus donations, scans, hacks, sabotage, smuggling and on-foot (Odyssey) work — each with its own checklist and tailored advice.

Flight safety & ship status

The operator watches the game's real-time status feed. A compact strip shows your fuel gauge and live hazards, and it speaks the ones that matter: "interdiction — submit and boost", overheating, shields down, low fuel. It reads out your docking pad number the instant control clears you, warns before a jump when the next star can't refuel you, and knows when you're deep in a menu so it won't nag you for "stalling" while you plan a route.

Knows your ship, mats & surveys

From your loadout it does the check the mission board never does: "that delivery won't fit your hold", "you don't have cabins for these VIPs", "you're not rigged to mine this". It keeps a running tally of your engineering materials and unlocked engineers, and while you explore it flags the bodies actually worth mapping — Earth-likes, water and ammonia worlds, terraformables — with an estimate of the survey data riding in your hold.

The operator one personality, and it does the talking

Proactive heartbeat

The operator notices when you stall: docked too long with hand-ins waiting, parked in a kill system without engaging, a contract about to expire. It tells you — out loud — and names the actual RES site or Nav Beacon it detected in-system. It keeps a miner company too: a greeting when you drop onto a ring, "that one's worth the limpets" on rich rocks, and tonnage milestones as the refinery fills — quietly, on strict cooldowns.

AI operator (optional)

Turn the AI on with one button — the app installs its own engine and model, with nothing else to download or configure — then ask it anything. "What should I do first?" gets an answer grounded in your real missions, system intel and expiry order, and it can read your live game data on the fly rather than guess (see below). Prefer to run your own? Point it at LM Studio instead, and a built-in advisor reads your RAM and graphics card and tells you which models actually fit.

Offline neural voices

Two bundled Piper voices speak briefings, warnings and stories — no cloud, ever. Six more voices are a one-click download away. Text never leaves your machine.

Ears (optional, off by default)

Talk to it. Hold CtrlShiftSpace — even with the game in focus — speak, release, and the operator answers out loud. Speech recognition runs on a local Whisper model (a one-time download on your click); your voice never leaves the machine. And it holds the thread: "and how far is that?" works, because the operator remembers what it just told you — including its own stories and warnings.

Eyes & a living copilot (optional, off by default)

With the AI turned on, the operator can glance at your screen and understand what you're doing — even in situations the journal doesn't record. It never reacts to a single frame in isolation: your undockings, jumps, interdictions, arrivals and hand-ins all stream into one running conversation alongside what it sees, so it rides the whole session with you and reacts in context — it knows the leg you just flew and the job you just closed, like a crewmate who's been there the whole time. Each screen is read into a short description first, so a remark lands on what's actually there — the target name, the ETA, the pad number — instead of guessing, and stays grounded even on small local models. Danger callouts stay instant; ambient beats are paced so it never floods you. The screenshot travels only to your own machine's LM endpoint and is never saved.

A memory

The operator remembers you across sessions: your history with every faction, every system you've visited (and where you lost a ship), personal records — richest contract, biggest bounty, best session. It brings memories up at the right moment ("that's your best session ever") and stays quiet the rest of the time — strict cooldowns make chatter-flooding impossible. On its first run it reads your recent journals, so it knows you from day one.

The Saga

When you log off, the operator narrates your session as a numbered space-opera episode — real events, real credits, real names, with continuity from the previous chapter and a cliffhanger for the next. Plus in-flight scuttlebutt about your passengers and the places on your route.

The panels the game keeps from you everything here exists because you cannot read it while flying

Route plotter — ship & fleet carrier (opt-in)

A plotter tab for the two routes the galaxy map won't give you. For your ship, the neutron highway — every supercharge star between here and there, plotted against your real jump range. For your fleet carrier, the thing the game refuses to do at all: the whole trip jump by jump, since a carrier hop is one system name typed in by hand and a run to Colonia is forty-six of them. Every stop has a copy button, and the next one lands on your clipboard automatically the moment you arrive — jump, paste, jump. And above the list, the number that decides whether the trip happens at all: the tritium. It reads your carrier's depot and tonnage from the journal, adds what you tell it is in the hold, and answers plainly — "short 2,739 t — seven runs with your 400 t hold" — flagging the stops with icy rings where you could mine it instead, and warning you when the load won't physically fit aboard. The operator follows the same route you do: it calls each waypoint out loud and knows about the shortfall before it advises you on anything.

Orrery — where everything is, right now

The system map, at the size of a HUD. Every scan you take carries the body's whole orbit — semi-major axis, eccentricity, inclination, periapsis, ascending node, mean anomaly and period — so the tab is not a picture of the system, it is the arithmetic: it places each body where it actually is at this instant, and a warp control runs the clock forward to show you where it will be. Nothing is simulated and nothing drifts, because every frame solves the same equations at a different time. Stars, planets, barycentres and belt clusters as the bands they are; the body you are standing on is ringed; tap anything for its distance, its orbital period and how eccentric it is. Planet distances are to scale — a leg of 1,900 ls draws at just under half a cluster sitting at 4,000, because that is the proportion you actually fly — and only within a planet's own satellite system are distances stretched, which is the only way a 1 ls moon stays visible at all. Bodies that would still land on top of each other are nudged apart until you can count them — a map is read to answer what is here, and two worlds drawn as one blob answer nothing; zooming in is what opens the knots. The card always says which mode it is in, with a switch to exact distances — untouched, nothing nudged — when you want to see the real thing. Every body is named — stars, planets, moons and barycentres — because you cannot look one up as "the green one". Each name takes the first free berth around its body, and one with nowhere to go waits rather than being stacked on top of a neighbour, so the map fills in with names as you scroll to zoom. Drag to pan, double-click to frame the whole system again.

Bodies are lit by the star that actually lights them — the terminator faces the real thing, so the day side on screen is the day side in the game — and their surfaces are generated, not downloaded: no texture files, nothing fetched, just the class the journal already told us. Ice reads as ice, gas giants get bands, metal-rich goes ochre, and the grain appears as you zoom in far enough to have a surface worth looking at. Landable worlds keep their green rim, because that is the one thing on the map you can act on.

Every dock you have visited is marked — stations, outposts, settlements and construction depots, beside the world they belong to. Docks are not bodies and never get scanned, so a surface port is placed on the world the journal names outright, and an orbital one is matched to what it orbits by distance from the arrival star, which both report: Anders City says 970.04 ls, its world says 970.0. What cannot be matched is left undrawn rather than parked beside the wrong planet.

Your ship is on the map too — as honestly as it can be. Elite reports no in-system position: the status file carries flags, fuel, cargo and your nav target, and nothing else. So instead of inventing a dot, the tab draws the leg you are flying — from where you dropped to supercruise toward whatever you have targeted — and shows your progress along it as a band rather than a point. The width is not decoration: measured across this commander's own journals the same 4,697 ls run took 137, 162 and 304 seconds on different days, and a 0.8 ls hop took as long as either, because a supercruise trip is spool-up and deceleration far more than it is distance. The band slides as you fly, and the moment you drop in it is replaced by fact. Parked, you are drawn too — docked or dropped at a station, a filled chevron marks the exact dock, filled because an arrival is a fact the journal stated where the in-flight marker is an estimate and says so. Flying toward a station you have never visited, the card tells you the destination is not mapped yet rather than showing nothing. And a recenter chip snaps the camera to your ship, parked or mid-leg, and keeps following it — while following, zoom anchors on the ship, so you can wheel in and out without the one marker you care about sliding off frame. In supercruise the camera flies the leg with you: wide at departure, when the destination is far and the frame holds most of the system, tightening continuously as you close, easing back out when the target leaves the map. Touch the wheel and it is yours. Grab the map and it lets go; the chip flips back to recenter, one tap from resuming.

The HUD switches tabs with the game. Opening a station market while a colonisation build is on the books brings up the architect's shopping list; undocking brings back the system map. The moment a commander cannot click the HUD is precisely the moment the game is being played, so the HUD stops waiting for the click — and a toggle in Settings turns the whole behaviour off. It only ever moves between tabs a click could reach: no build, or a system too bare to draw, switches nothing.

Tap a body and it opens its card. Distance from arrival and from its parent, orbital period, eccentricity, surface temperature, atmosphere, volcanism, whether it is tidally locked — and gravity in G, which turns amber past 2 and red past 3, because that is the number that writes off ships. If it is landable you also get its surface materials, richest first, and each one annotated with how many you already carry, because the app has been tracking your grid all along. Rarity does the shouting rather than the percentage: iron at 21% stays grey when you are holding 300 of it, while tellurium at 1% with none held is the line that tells you to land. First footfall is flagged too. It draws only what you have scanned: no third-party database, no bodies guessed into position. And it counts every scan you have ever taken, not just tonight's — arrive in a system you surveyed a year ago and the map is already complete, because the app reads your whole journal history for that system rather than asking you to do the FSS twice. One real system: 126 scans spread across 510 journal files, found in under a second, drawn as 36 bodies and 12 ring bands.

Comms traffic — the channel other people are on (new in 1.1.0)

Not the operator. Other people: traffic control working a queue, hauliers on the open channel, a fleet carrier broadcasting at everyone, your own crew on the intercom. Mostly they do not care that you exist, which is exactly what makes the ones who do land. A transmission is a scene, not a line — a call and a response, because two voices imply two people who exist whether or not you are listening.

Everything it states is true. Every scene is built from a brief: the exact set of names and figures it is allowed to use, each tagged with where it came from. A haulier grumbling that they have “taken another 380 off Bertrandite at Hurston Ring” is quoting your own market memory — a price you actually saw, at a station you actually docked at. Anything the AI writes is checked back against that brief before it is spoken, and a scene that reached for a faction, a station or a number nobody licensed is thrown away whole. So the chatter doubles as intelligence, and silence is always the safe failure. Old prices are spoken as old — “last I looked, three days back” — and anything past a week is not mentioned at all.

Range is real. Station traffic is gated on the actual distance to the port, taken from the orrery, and fades in as you close — so a distant station sounds distant, and a port the app cannot place stays silent rather than being invented. Deep space is not a distance threshold; it is what is left when nobody is in reach: a carrier tone, your own crew, and a great deal of nothing.

Voices come back. A callsign heard in a system is remembered, keeps the same voice, and turns up again next time you are there. Ships you actually hear on the game's own comms can be enrolled into that cast, so the fiction has a spine of things that genuinely happened. Threads run across sessions — something set up gets complicated, and eventually turns.

It knows when to shut up. Chatter thins as the pressure rises, which is the exact opposite of what the operator does. In a firefight or a hull emergency every ambient channel goes silent — the sudden absence of noise you had stopped noticing is the cheapest tension in the app, and it costs nothing to generate. The operator keeps talking through all of it; that contrast is the point.

And the model keeps writing. A fixed set of lines eventually repeats itself — however many are authored, the rotation is finite. So your local AI writes fresh scenes continuously, for whichever channels are live, into the same slots the event triggers use. The bundled templates become the floor rather than the ceiling: what you hear when the model is busy, absent, or wrote something that failed its fact check. How busy the air is has its own setting — sparse, normal, busy or bustling — and the default works out around one transmission every forty seconds in a system with ports in it.

And it cycles rather than shuffles. Lines are chosen least-recently-used first, never at random — random picking clusters, so with any finite set you hear some lines three times before others get an airing once. Measured across an hour at the shipped density: 88% of transmissions are distinct, nothing plays more than twice, and nothing comes back inside nineteen minutes. That ordering is remembered across restarts, so every session does not open with the same three lines.

The Comms tab lists every channel, its signal strength, and — when one is shut — why. Every transmission is logged with its speakers, so the whole thing works with the sound off, and anything carrying reported intelligence is marked with its sources one tap away. Repetitive? The template file is plain text and yours to extend — declare your own ship names and lines and they join the rotation. Off by default.

The radio (new in 1.1.0)

Every spoken word now runs through an actual radio channel — the operator, the wire, the saga and the traffic alike. A bandpass into the telephone band, a little soft-clip saturation, a noise bed that opens and closes with the transmission, the odd crackle, a squelch gap before speech and a roger beep after it. Each channel has its own character, and distance degrades it: the same voice from a station two thousand light seconds out is narrower, hissier and breaking up.

Underneath there are two buses, and the split is the important part. The operator is on priority; ambient traffic is on ambient, which ducks about 14 dB underneath it and drops rather than queues when it backs up. A dock worker's joke can never come between you and a hull-breach callout, and a transmission that has gone stale is discarded instead of arriving four minutes late.

System architect — the build, as a shopping list

Dock at a construction site and the whole requirement comes across into its own tab. The game states it once, on the contribution panel, as an alphabetical wall of required / provided — and you cannot read it at all once you undock to go shopping. Here it becomes an ordered plan: what is already in your hold that the site wants (hand it over before you fly anywhere), then what is on sale at this station, then everything for sale in the build's own system — no jump, just supercruise — and only then the wider galaxy. Each line opens to show where to buy it, at what price, how much they hold, and how old that report is, because a two-month-old listing is a rumour and the panel says so in red. Lines are grouped by seller, so the stop that clears the most of the build comes first — one real site wanted 6,703 t across seventeen commodities, and two stations in its own system covered 99% of it. Every docking teaches it more: read a market and that reading replaces anyone else's report about the same station, and a shelf you found empty is never suggested again. It also counts the trips against your actual hold — "×6" next to a line is six full loads — and credits your deliveries the moment you make them.

The local wire — news for the system you are in (new in 1.0.3)

Galnet reports the galaxy. Nothing reports the system you are standing in — who runs it, who is gaining on them, what the construction sites are short of, what steel did this week. So your own AI writes the local paper, from a brief of things the journal says are true right now: the faction board with real influence figures, the stations, the signals, the markets you have read, the doors that have been shut in your face. Six desks take turns — civic, industry, economy, crime, sport and life — so it is a paper and not an almanac. The economy desk is a real market report: “Steel at Niinimäki is 3,905 cr, up 13% since it was last read”, the spread between two stations, who is paying and how badly. And it remembers what it invented: the dock-crew league keeps the same two teams from one edition to the next, the same barman turns up in the same bar. It may invent people, fixtures and gossip; it may not invent a faction, a station or a price — anything it makes up that looks like a real place is thrown away unprinted. Choose how often it files, from every ten minutes to hourly, or off with a button to run one on demand.

Trade leads & profitable runs

Every commodities market you open is remembered, and when a buy-low / sell-high spread clears your threshold a dismissible trade lead card appears. Ask what's worth hauling and it goes further: it searches community market data for the best buy-here, sell-there run out of your system, sized to your hold and filtered to pads your ship can actually land on. The answer arrives as a card with the numbers you act on — credits a ton, credits a full hold, how far — and a copy button on every destination, so the system name is on your clipboard ready for a Ctrl+V in the galaxy map. A multi-hop Spansh loop is one question away when you want one.

Exobiology, worked out for you

Odyssey makes you take three samples of a species, each one far enough from the last — and never tells you how far. The operator does: 500 m for Bacterium, 800 m for Osseus, called out the moment you take a sample, and then a single "far enough" when you've walked it. Before you commit to a landing it tells you what's down there, roughly what a full set is worth, and how much walking it will cost. If nobody has logged that body before, it says so — a first sample pays five times as much. And when you cash it all in at Vista Genomics, the payout goes into your session ledger and your records, bonuses included.

What it will go and fetch only when you ask, and only what the question needs

Reads your live data on the fly

Ask "what's worth buying here?" and the operator doesn't guess — it reads the live commodities market in front of you before it answers. With a tool-calling model it can reach for exactly what a question needs: the current market, where to buy or sell a commodity across the stations you've visited, a profitable run out of here, a multi-hop Spansh loop, whether cargo fits your hold, your missions, materials or system intel. No more being sent to buy something that sold out hours ago — and if your model can't call tools, it still answers grounded in the market it can see. All local; nothing leaves your machine.

The wider galaxy, on request (opt-in)

Three community sources the operator touches only when you ask it to. "Any news?" reads the Galnet wire and passes the stories on as news — not as something you were there for. "Where can I sell this?" searches markets across the galaxy (Ardent Insight, built from shared EDDN data), not only the stations you have visited. And "is that system worth the detour?" checks the exploration catalogue for what other commanders have already found there, down to which species are logged on which body — and when it comes back empty it says that means nobody has been yet, not that there is nothing to find. Each is off until you switch it on, and each sends the bare minimum: a system name, a commodity, or nothing at all.

Installation — step by step

novice friendly

Download the installer

Click the download button above — you get a single file, ED-Mission-Operator-1.1.0-setup.exe (about 132 MB — it carries the offline voices). Your browser may ask where to save it; Downloads is fine.

Run it — and get past the blue warning

Double-click the downloaded file. Windows will very likely show a blue "Windows protected your PC" screen. That's SmartScreen being cautious about a small hobby app that isn't code-signed — not a virus detection.

SmartScreen — what to click Click "More info" (small text, middle of the dialog) → then the "Run anyway" button appears. Click it.

The installer needs no choices and no administrator password — it installs only for your user account and starts the app when done.

Meet the HUD

A small dark panel appears and floats above other windows — that's the operator. Drag it by its title bar to wherever suits your cockpit. It reads "Journal connected" at the bottom when it has found your game files (automatic — no setup needed if Elite has ever run on this PC).

Start Elite Dangerous — in Borderless mode

In the game: Options → Graphics → Display → Borderless (a true Fullscreen display hides all overlays — this is a Windows rule, not the app's). Your active missions appear on the HUD by themselves; accept a new one at any mission board and the operator will introduce it, out loud.

Turn on the AI operator — one button

Everything above already works. To give the operator its voice, its stories and its eyes, open Settings ⚙ → AI engine, choose "This app (no other software needed)", and press the button. Nothing else to install — the app fetches a small inference runtime and a model, picks the right one for your graphics card, and runs it all on this machine.

The download is about 4–6 GB depending on the model your card can take, one time, and it resumes if you cancel or lose connection. Progress shows in Settings; the HUD says "Local AI engine ready" when it's serving.

Already run LM Studio? Keep it. Pick "LM Studio (run it yourself)" in the same dropdown and point the endpoint at it as before — no second copy of a model needed. It's also the fallback if a graphics driver ever argues with the built-in engine.

The model choice is made for you, and it's a Gemma: a vision model that grounds its talk in your real game data — the job, the payout, the deadline. In testing, bigger or roleplay-tuned models sound colourful but invent lore (station reputations and history that aren't there), so more parameters isn't better here.

Voices & taste

The operator speaks out of the box (Alba, a Scottish neural voice — fully offline). In Settings ⚙ you can switch to the bundled male voice, download six more with one click, adjust rate and volume, tune how chatty the storytelling is — or mute everything with the 🔊 button.

Want to talk back? Tick Voice input in the same Voice section and grab the one-time speech-recognition download (~150 MB, fully local). Then hold CtrlShiftSpace — even mid-flight — speak, release, and the operator answers.

If you ever want it gone

Windows Settings → Apps → Installed apps → ED Mission Operator → Uninstall. It leaves your game and its files untouched (it never wrote to them in the first place).

Privacy & fair play

flight rules

● What it will never do

  • No cloud, no account, no telemetry. The AI runs on your own machine — all inference traffic is to 127.0.0.1, whether that is the built-in engine or your own LM Studio. The only downloads are ones you click: the AI engine and its model (from their official sources), extra voices, and four opt-in community lookups — all off by default. Spansh routes and Ardent market searches send just a system name (and the commodity you asked about); the exploration catalogue sends only the system name; Galnet sends nothing at all.
  • Memory stays home. The operator's long-term memory is one local JSON file you can open, inspect, or wipe with one click ("Forget everything"). Screen glances are off by default; when enabled, screenshots go only to your local LM endpoint and are never written to disk.
  • Your voice stays home. Push-to-talk is off by default; when enabled, speech is transcribed by a local Whisper model on your own CPU. No cloud speech service, ever — the microphone is only live while you hold the key.
  • Open source. Don't take any of this on faith — the full source is on GitHub (MIT license): the tested TypeScript mission engine, the Rust shell, the prompts, everything. Build it yourself with Node + Rust, or read the network code for yourself — every request the app can make is in the Rust shell, and each one is named on this page.
  • Read-only. It reads the journal text files Frontier writes for third-party tools; it never writes to the game's folders.
  • No game hooks. It never touches game memory, injects code, or talks to game servers — the same mechanism trusted community tools have used for years.
  • Your words stay home. The bundled voices synthesize speech locally; nothing you read or hear leaves your PC.

Does it need the internet?

No. Missions, voices, leads and stories all work offline. Internet is only touched for the one-time AI engine + model download (or LM Studio models if you use that instead), extra voices, or the optional community lookups — Spansh routes, galaxy-wide markets, the exploration catalogue, Galnet news — each on your explicit click, all off by default.

Will it cost me frames?

The HUD itself is negligible. AI answers use your GPU for a few seconds when generated — and the built-in advisor deliberately recommends models that leave the game's share of the graphics card alone.

Which game modes?

Open, private groups and solo — it only reads what the game writes locally, so it works identically everywhere, including Horizons and Odyssey, on foot or in a ring.

Something broke?

The HUD degrades politely: no AI set up → deterministic guidance; no voice → text; no game running → it waits. Restart the app and it rebuilds everything from the journal.
