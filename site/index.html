A companion tool for Elite Dangerous commanders

A mission HUD
with a voice.

A small always-on-top panel that reads your Player Journal live: mission cards with objectives and timers, a proactive operator that nudges you when you stall, and an AI wingman that answers out loud in offline neural voices. It watches your ship in real time — fuel, heat, shields, interdictions, your docking pad — checks whether your hold and cabins can carry a job before you fly it, finds you a profitable trade run, does the exobiology arithmetic while you walk, and writes the session up as a space-opera episode when you log off. It remembers you across sessions — your factions, your records, where you lost a ship — and you can talk to it: hold a key, speak, and it answers. Give it a vision model and it turns copilot, riding the whole session with you. Everything runs on your machine — including the AI, which it installs itself.

01

What it does

module manifest
A

Live mission tracking

Reads the game's own journal files as you play. Cards show category, destination, reward, kill and cargo counters, gift commodities for VIPs, and a synthesized objective checklist the game never gives you — with live expiry timers. It reads the whole board: couriers and cargo, passengers and sightseeing, massacres and assassinations, plus donations, scans, hacks, sabotage, smuggling and on-foot (Odyssey) work — each with its own checklist and tailored advice.

B

Proactive heartbeat

The operator notices when you stall: docked too long with hand-ins waiting, parked in a kill system without engaging, a contract about to expire. It tells you — out loud — and names the actual RES site or Nav Beacon it detected in-system. It keeps a miner company too: a greeting when you drop onto a ring, "that one's worth the limpets" on rich rocks, and tonnage milestones as the refinery fills — quietly, on strict cooldowns.

C

AI operator (optional)

Turn the AI on with one button — the app installs its own engine and model, with nothing else to download or configure — then ask it anything. "What should I do first?" gets an answer grounded in your real missions, system intel and expiry order, and it can read your live game data on the fly rather than guess (see below). Prefer to run your own? Point it at LM Studio instead, and a built-in advisor reads your RAM and graphics card and tells you which models actually fit.

D

Offline neural voices

Two bundled Piper voices speak briefings, warnings and stories — no cloud, ever. Six more voices are a one-click download away. Text never leaves your machine.

E

Trade leads & profitable runs

Every commodities market you open is remembered, and when a buy-low / sell-high spread clears your threshold a dismissible trade lead card appears. Ask what's worth hauling and it goes further: it searches community market data for the best buy-here, sell-there run out of your system, sized to your hold and filtered to pads your ship can actually land on. The answer arrives as a card with the numbers you act on — credits a ton, credits a full hold, how far — and a copy button on every destination, so the system name is on your clipboard ready for a Ctrl+V in the galaxy map. A multi-hop Spansh loop is one question away when you want one.

F

The Saga

When you log off, the operator narrates your session as a numbered space-opera episode — real events, real credits, real names, with continuity from the previous chapter and a cliffhanger for the next. Plus in-flight scuttlebutt about your passengers and the places on your route.

G

A memory

The operator remembers you across sessions: your history with every faction, every system you've visited (and where you lost a ship), personal records — richest contract, biggest bounty, best session. It brings memories up at the right moment ("that's your best session ever") and stays quiet the rest of the time — strict cooldowns make chatter-flooding impossible. On its first run it reads your recent journals, so it knows you from day one.

H

Eyes & a living copilot (optional, off by default)

With the AI turned on, the operator can glance at your screen and understand what you're doing — even in situations the journal doesn't record. It never reacts to a single frame in isolation: your undockings, jumps, interdictions, arrivals and hand-ins all stream into one running conversation alongside what it sees, so it rides the whole session with you and reacts in context — it knows the leg you just flew and the job you just closed, like a crewmate who's been there the whole time. Each screen is read into a short description first, so a remark lands on what's actually there — the target name, the ETA, the pad number — instead of guessing, and stays grounded even on small local models. Danger callouts stay instant; ambient beats are paced so it never floods you. The screenshot travels only to your own machine's LM endpoint and is never saved.

I

Ears (optional, off by default)

Talk to it. Hold CtrlShiftSpace — even with the game in focus — speak, release, and the operator answers out loud. Speech recognition runs on a local Whisper model (a one-time download on your click); your voice never leaves the machine. And it holds the thread: "and how far is that?" works, because the operator remembers what it just told you — including its own stories and warnings.

J

Flight safety & ship status

The operator watches the game's real-time status feed. A compact strip shows your fuel gauge and live hazards, and it speaks the ones that matter: "interdiction — submit and boost", overheating, shields down, low fuel. It reads out your docking pad number the instant control clears you, warns before a jump when the next star can't refuel you, and knows when you're deep in a menu so it won't nag you for "stalling" while you plan a route.

K

Knows your ship, mats & surveys

From your loadout it does the check the mission board never does: "that delivery won't fit your hold", "you don't have cabins for these VIPs", "you're not rigged to mine this". It keeps a running tally of your engineering materials and unlocked engineers, and while you explore it flags the bodies actually worth mapping — Earth-likes, water and ammonia worlds, terraformables — with an estimate of the survey data riding in your hold.

L

Reads your live data on the fly

Ask "what's worth buying here?" and the operator doesn't guess — it reads the live commodities market in front of you before it answers. With a tool-calling model it can reach for exactly what a question needs: the current market, where to buy or sell a commodity across the stations you've visited, a profitable run out of here, a multi-hop Spansh loop, whether cargo fits your hold, your missions, materials or system intel. No more being sent to buy something that sold out hours ago — and if your model can't call tools, it still answers grounded in the market it can see. All local; nothing leaves your machine.

M

Exobiology, worked out for you

Odyssey makes you take three samples of a species, each one far enough from the last — and never tells you how far. The operator does: 500 m for Bacterium, 800 m for Osseus, called out the moment you take a sample, and then a single "far enough" when you've walked it. Before you commit to a landing it tells you what's down there, roughly what a full set is worth, and how much walking it will cost. If nobody has logged that body before, it says so — a first sample pays five times as much. And when you cash it all in at Vista Genomics, the payout goes into your session ledger and your records, bonuses included.

N

The wider galaxy, on request (opt-in)

Three community sources the operator touches only when you ask it to. "Any news?" reads the Galnet wire and passes the stories on as news — not as something you were there for. "Where can I sell this?" searches markets across the galaxy (Ardent Insight, built from shared EDDN data), not only the stations you have visited. And "is that system worth the detour?" checks the exploration catalogue for what other commanders have already found there, down to which species are logged on which body — and when it comes back empty it says that means nobody has been yet, not that there is nothing to find. Each is off until you switch it on, and each sends the bare minimum: a system name, a commodity, or nothing at all.

04

Installation — step by step

novice friendly

Download the installer

Click the download button above — you get a single file, ED-Mission-Operator-1.0.0-setup.exe (about 131 MB — it carries the offline voices). Your browser may ask where to save it; Downloads is fine.

Run it — and get past the blue warning

Double-click the downloaded file. Windows will very likely show a blue "Windows protected your PC" screen. That's SmartScreen being cautious about a small hobby app that isn't code-signed — not a virus detection.

SmartScreen — what to click Click "More info" (small text, middle of the dialog) → then the "Run anyway" button appears. Click it.

The installer needs no choices and no administrator password — it installs only for your user account and starts the app when done.

Meet the HUD

A small dark panel appears and floats above other windows — that's the operator. Drag it by its title bar to wherever suits your cockpit. It reads "Journal connected" at the bottom when it has found your game files (automatic — no setup needed if Elite has ever run on this PC).

Start Elite Dangerous — in Borderless mode

In the game: Options → Graphics → Display → Borderless (a true Fullscreen display hides all overlays — this is a Windows rule, not the app's). Your active missions appear on the HUD by themselves; accept a new one at any mission board and the operator will introduce it, out loud.

Turn on the AI operator — one button

Everything above already works. To give the operator its voice, its stories and its eyes, open Settings ⚙ → AI engine, choose "This app (no other software needed)", and press the button. Nothing else to install — the app fetches a small inference runtime and a model, picks the right one for your graphics card, and runs it all on this machine.

The download is about 4–6 GB depending on the model your card can take, one time, and it resumes if you cancel or lose connection. Progress shows in Settings; the HUD says "Local AI engine ready" when it's serving.

Already run LM Studio? Keep it. Pick "LM Studio (run it yourself)" in the same dropdown and point the endpoint at it as before — no second copy of a model needed. It's also the fallback if a graphics driver ever argues with the built-in engine.

The model choice is made for you, and it's a Gemma: a vision model that grounds its talk in your real game data — the job, the payout, the deadline. In testing, bigger or roleplay-tuned models sound colourful but invent lore (station reputations and history that aren't there), so more parameters isn't better here.

Voices & taste

The operator speaks out of the box (Alba, a Scottish neural voice — fully offline). In Settings ⚙ you can switch to the bundled male voice, download six more with one click, adjust rate and volume, tune how chatty the storytelling is — or mute everything with the 🔊 button.

Want to talk back? Tick Voice input in the same Voice section and grab the one-time speech-recognition download (~150 MB, fully local). Then hold CtrlShiftSpace — even mid-flight — speak, release, and the operator answers.

If you ever want it gone

Windows Settings → Apps → Installed apps → ED Mission Operator → Uninstall. It leaves your game and its files untouched (it never wrote to them in the first place).

06

Privacy & fair play

flight rules

● What it will never do

  • No cloud, no account, no telemetry. The AI runs on your own machine — all inference traffic is to 127.0.0.1, whether that is the built-in engine or your own LM Studio. The only downloads are ones you click: the AI engine and its model (from their official sources), extra voices, and four opt-in community lookups — all off by default. Spansh routes and Ardent market searches send just a system name (and the commodity you asked about); the exploration catalogue sends only the system name; Galnet sends nothing at all.
  • Memory stays home. The operator's long-term memory is one local JSON file you can open, inspect, or wipe with one click ("Forget everything"). Screen glances are off by default; when enabled, screenshots go only to your local LM endpoint and are never written to disk.
  • Your voice stays home. Push-to-talk is off by default; when enabled, speech is transcribed by a local Whisper model on your own CPU. No cloud speech service, ever — the microphone is only live while you hold the key.
  • Open source. Don't take any of this on faith — the full source is on GitHub (MIT license): the tested TypeScript mission engine, the Rust shell, the prompts, everything. Build it yourself with Node + Rust, or read the network code for yourself — every request the app can make is in the Rust shell, and each one is named on this page.
  • Read-only. It reads the journal text files Frontier writes for third-party tools; it never writes to the game's folders.
  • No game hooks. It never touches game memory, injects code, or talks to game servers — the same mechanism trusted community tools have used for years.
  • Your words stay home. The bundled voices synthesize speech locally; nothing you read or hear leaves your PC.

Does it need the internet?

No. Missions, voices, leads and stories all work offline. Internet is only touched for the one-time AI engine + model download (or LM Studio models if you use that instead), extra voices, or the optional community lookups — Spansh routes, galaxy-wide markets, the exploration catalogue, Galnet news — each on your explicit click, all off by default.

Will it cost me frames?

The HUD itself is negligible. AI answers use your GPU for a few seconds when generated — and the built-in advisor deliberately recommends models that leave the game's share of the graphics card alone.

Which game modes?

Open, private groups and solo — it only reads what the game writes locally, so it works identically everywhere, including Horizons and Odyssey, on foot or in a ring.

Something broke?

The HUD degrades politely: no AI set up → deterministic guidance; no voice → text; no game running → it waits. Restart the app and it rebuilds everything from the journal.
